So today we are going to talk about the central limit theorem, which is honestly one of the most important results in the whole of statistics. Here is the key idea. If you take the average of a large number of independent random variables, that average is approximately normally distributed, no matter what distribution you started with. Let me say that again because it is the whole point of the lecture. It does not matter what the original distribution looks like. It could be uniform, it could be exponential, it could be something horrible and skewed. The average of many of them looks like a bell curve. Now, um, where was I. Yes. Why does this matter? It matters because in the real world we almost never know the true distribution of our data. But the central limit theorem tells us we do not need to. As long as we have enough samples, we can use the normal distribution for our confidence intervals and our hypothesis tests. That is why the normal distribution is everywhere. Now let us be a bit more precise. Suppose X one up to X n are independent and identically distributed, with mean mu and variance sigma squared. Then the sample mean, minus mu, divided by sigma over root n, converges in distribution to a standard normal as n goes to infinity. The crucial factor here is the root n. The spread of the sample mean shrinks like one over root n. So to halve your error, you need four times as many samples. That is a classic exam question, so write it down. Okay, a quick aside, I was at a conference last week and someone had a very nice slide about this, I will try to find it. Anyway. Let me give you an intuition for why it works. Think about rolling a single die. The distribution is flat, every face is equally likely. Now roll two dice and add them. Suddenly seven is the most likely total, and two and twelve are rare, because there are more ways to make seven. Roll ten dice and the histogram of the total is already a beautiful bell shape. Every time you add another independent variable you are smoothing the distribution a bit more. Now, a warning. The theorem needs finite variance. If your data come from a heavy tailed distribution like the Cauchy distribution, the central limit theorem fails completely, and the average of a million samples is just as wild as a single sample. So always check your tails. That is where people get caught out in practice. Right, I think we will stop there for today, and next time we will do confidence intervals properly. Thank you.
